Measurement & Math Toolkit

Chemistry is a quantitative science. Before anything else, you need the four math tools every AP problem uses: significant figures, scientific notation, percent error, and unit conversion.

🔢 Significant Figures — the rules

  • Non-zero digits always count. 457 → 3 sig figs
  • Sandwiched zeros (between non-zeros) count. 2001 → 4
  • Leading zeros never count. 0.0052 → 2
  • Trailing zeros count only with a decimal point. 100 → 1, but 100. → 3 and 1.00×10³ → 3
Multiplying / dividing → answer keeps the fewest sig figs.
Adding / subtracting → answer keeps the fewest decimal places.

Atoms, Isotopes & Ions

Three particles build everything: protons (+, define the element), neutrons (neutral, add mass), and electrons (−, do all the chemistry).

🏷️ Isotopic Notation — read the label

Amass # = p⁺ + n⁰ Zatomic # = p⁺ X ±ccharge = p⁺ − e⁻
3717Cl → 17 protons, 37 − 17 = 20 neutrons, 17 electrons
118Sn2+ → 50 p⁺, 68 n⁰, 50 − 2 = 48 e⁻
Isotopes = same protons, different neutrons.
Ions = same protons, different electrons. Lose e⁻ → cation (+). Gain e⁻ → anion (−).

⚖️ Average Atomic Mass

The periodic table mass is a weighted average of natural isotopes:

avg mass = Σ (fraction × isotope mass)
Copper: 69.2% at 62.93 amu + 30.8% at 64.93 amu
(0.692)(62.93) + (0.308)(64.93) = 43.55 + 20.00 = 63.55 amu ✓ matches the table!
The average always lands closest to the most abundant isotope. Use that to sanity-check.

Light & Electron Configuration

Electrons live in energy levels. When they jump down, they emit light — and two equations connect everything.

🌊 Waves: the two master equations

c = λν speed = wavelength × frequency
c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s
E = hν energy = Planck × frequency
h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
E = hc/λ combine them:
short wavelength ⇒ high energy
radiomicroIRvisibleUVX-rayγ

← longer λ, lower E  |  shorter λ, higher E →

🪜 Electron Configuration — the filling order

Fill lowest energy first (Aufbau): 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p 6s…

Fe (26 e⁻): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶
Na⁺ (10 e⁻): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ — lost one, now looks like Ne
O²⁻ (10 e⁻): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ — gained two, also looks like Ne
Ions chase a noble-gas configuration. That’s the whole reason charges are predictable.

Replace the inner core with the previous noble gas in brackets:

Cs: [Xe] 6s¹
Ga: [Ar] 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p¹
Xe: [Kr] 5s² 4d¹⁰ 5p⁶

06

Periodicity — the three trends

One idea explains all three trends: effective nuclear pull vs. distance. More protons pull harder; more shells put valence electrons farther away.

→ across a period: radius shrinks, IE & EN rise (more protons, same shell)
↓ down a group: radius grows, IE & EN fall (more shells, more shielding)
F = most electronegative ✨

📏 Atomic Radius

Distance from nucleus to outer electrons.

K > Na > Li (more shells)
Li > Be > … > F (more pull, same shell)

⚡ Ionization Energy

Energy needed to remove an electron.

Small atom = held tight = high IE.
Cl > S > P;  Ca lowest of Be/Mg/Ca

🧲 Electronegativity

How strongly an atom pulls shared electrons in a bond.

F is the champion.
K weakest of Li/Na/K.

🧮 Writing ionic formulas: the criss-cross

Ca2+ PO₄3− → Ca₃(PO₄)₂

Charges swap and become subscripts. Polyatomics with a subscript get parentheses.

📝 Pattern cheat sheet

  • per___ate → one more O than -ate (ClO₄⁻ perchlorate)
  • ___ate → the base form (ClO₃⁻ chlorate)
  • ___ite → one less O (ClO₂⁻ chlorite)
  • hypo___ite → two less O (ClO⁻ hypochlorite)
  • -ide → usually a single element anion (chloride, oxide, nitride)

Equations & Reaction Types

Balance with coefficients (never change subscripts!), then classify the pattern.

Synthesis

A + B → AB

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Decomposition

AB → A + B

2KNO₃ → 2KNO₂ + O₂

Single Replacement

A + BC → AC + B

Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂

Double Replacement

AB + CD → AD + CB

AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃

Combustion

CₓHᵧ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Acid–Base

HA + BOH → BA + H₂O

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

💧 Net Ionic Equations in 3 steps

  1. Molecular: Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq)
  2. Complete ionic: split everything (aq) into ions; solids/liquids/gases stay whole:
    Pb²⁺ + 2NO₃⁻ + 2K⁺ + 2I⁻ → PbI₂(s) + 2K⁺ + 2NO₃⁻
  3. Cancel spectators (K⁺, NO₃⁻ appear unchanged on both sides):
    Pb²⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s)

⚖️ Limiting Reactant — the recipe rule

  1. Convert both reactant masses to moles.
  2. Divide each by its coefficient — the smaller result is limiting.
  3. Use the limiting reactant’s moles (and mole ratios) to find the product.
% yield = actualtheoretical × 100 — you can collect at most what the limiting reactant allows.

Acids & Bases

Brønsted–Lowry: an acid donates a proton (H⁺); a base accepts one. That’s it. Follow the H⁺.

🌈 The pH scale

stomach acid soda pure water (7) baking soda drain cleaner
0 — acid (sour, ↑H⁺)7 — neutral14 — base (bitter, ↑OH⁻)

👯 Conjugate Pairs — track the proton

acid HNO₂  +  base H₂O  ⇌  conj. acid H₃O⁺  +  conj. base NO₂⁻
  • The acid loses H⁺ → becomes the conjugate base.
  • The base gains H⁺ → becomes the conjugate acid.
  • Pairs differ by exactly one H⁺: HNO₂/NO₂⁻ and H₂O/H₃O⁺.

✍️ Completing acid–base reactions

Move one H⁺ from the acid to the base, write both products:

HF + CH₃COO⁻ → F⁻ + CH₃COOH
H₂S + CO₃²⁻ → HS⁻ + HCO₃⁻
NH₄⁺ + F⁻ → NH₃ + HF
Amphoteric species (like H₂O or HSO₄⁻) can play either role depending on the partner.

Dimensional Analysis

7a. A 5-carat diamond — how many pennyweights?
5 carat × (0.200 g / 1 carat) × (1 grain / 0.0648 g) × (1 pwt / 24 grain) = 1.00 g → 15.43 grain → 0.643 pwt
7b. Mass of 2.3 troy oz of gold in grams?
2.3 troy oz × (20 pwt / 1 oz) × (24 grain / 1 pwt) × (0.0648 g / 1 grain) = 46 × 24 × 0.0648 = 72 g (2 sf; unrounded 71.5 g)
7c. Volume of 1 troy pound of gold (ρ = 19.3 g/cm³)?
1 troy lb = 12 oz × 20 pwt × 24 grain = 5760 grain × 0.0648 g = 373.2 g.
V = m/ρ = 373.2 / 19.3 = 19.3 cm³
8a. An aspirin tablet has 5.00 × 10² mg of active ingredient. How many grains?
500. mg = 0.500 g × (1 grain / 0.0648 g) = 7.72 grains
8b. …and how many scruples?
7.72 grain × (1 scruple / 20 grain) = 0.386 scruple
8c. Mass of 1.00 scruple in grams?
1.00 scruple = 20 grain × 0.0648 g/grain = 1.30 g (check: 1 dram = 3 scruples = 3.888 g → 1.296 g ✓)
9. 100 m in 9.79 s — how long to run a mile?
Speed = 100 m / 9.79 s. A mile is 5280 ft (the sheet’s “5,820” is a typo) = 5280 × 12 in × 2.54 cm = 1609 m.
t = 1609 m × (9.79 s / 100 m) = 158 s ≈ 2.6 min   (if you use the printed 5,820 ft = 1774 m → 174 s)
10. 32 mi/gal vs 14 km/L — which is better? (1 gal = 4 qt, 1.057 qt = 1 L)
Convert 14 km/L: 14 km × (1 mi / 1.609 km) = 8.70 mi/L; 1 gal = 4 qt × (1 L / 1.057 qt) = 3.784 L.
8.70 mi/L × 3.784 L/gal = 32.9 mi/gal → your spouse’s car wins (barely)
11a. “New York — 112 km” at 65 mi/hr: how long?
112 km × (1 mi / 1.609 km) = 69.6 mi; t = 69.6 / 65 = 1.1 hr (about 64 min)
11b. At 28 mi/gal, how many liters for 112 km?
69.6 mi ÷ 28 mi/gal = 2.49 gal × 4 qt × (1 L / 1.057 qt) = 9.4 L

Classifying Matter

12. Differences between solid, liquid, gas (+ particle diagrams)?
Solid: particles tightly packed in a fixed lattice, only vibrate → definite shape & volume.
Liquid: particles close but slide past each other → definite volume, takes container’s shape.
Gas: particles far apart, moving fast and independently → fills any container (no fixed shape or volume).
Use the live simulation in Chapter 2 as your particle diagram reference!
13. Homogeneous vs heterogeneous?
Homogeneous: uniform composition throughout — every sample is identical (a solution).
Heterogeneous: visibly distinct regions/phases with different compositions.
14. Classify each:
a. soil — heterogeneous   b. atmosphere — homogeneous (clean air is a uniform solution of gases)   c. carbonated soda — heterogeneous (gas bubbles = second phase)   d. gasoline — homogeneous   e. gold — homogeneous (pure substance)   f. ethanol + water — homogeneous
15. Mixture or pure substance (element / compound)?
a. water — pure substance, compound   b. blood — mixture   c. oceans — mixture
d. iron — pure substance, element   e. brass — mixture (Cu + Zn alloy)   f. uranium — pure substance, element
16. Physical vs chemical changes?
Physical: the substance changes form/state but stays the same substance (melting, dissolving).
Chemical: bonds break and form — a new substance with new properties is produced (burning, rusting).
19. Four signs a chemical reaction occurred:
① color change  ② gas produced (bubbles/odor)  ③ precipitate (solid) forms  ④ energy change — heat or light released/absorbed
20. Glass rod over a burning candle turns black. For the wax, wick, and rod — physical, chemical, both, neither?
Wax — both: it melts (physical) and combusts into CO₂ + H₂O (chemical).
Wick — chemical: it burns, forming new substances (char, CO₂).
Glass rod — neither: the black is soot (carbon from incomplete combustion) deposited on the glass; the glass itself is unchanged.

Periodicity

37. Atomic radius:
a. Definition: the distance from the nucleus to the outermost electrons (half the distance between bonded identical nuclei).
b. Attraction between opposite charges weakens as distance increases.
c. More protons → stronger pull on valence electrons (same shell).
d. K is largest — most energy levels/shells of Li, Na, K.
e. As is smallest — same period as Ca and Cu, but the most protons pulling the same shells in tighter.
f. Agree. F and O share a period but F has more protons (smaller); Cl has an extra shell (bigger). So F is smallest.
38. Ionization energy:
a. Definition: the energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom.
b. Ca smallest — largest atom of the three, so its valence electron is farthest and most shielded → easiest to remove.
c. Cl largest — same period, most protons, smallest radius → holds electrons tightest.
d. Disagree. P has the extra shell, so P has the smallest IE; N actually has the largest of C, N, P.
39. Electronegativity:
a. Definition: an atom’s ability to attract shared electrons in a chemical bond.
b. F — the most electronegative element of all (top-right corner).
c. K — biggest atom of Li/Na/K, weakest hold on bonding electrons.
d. F — beats O (fewer protons) and Cl (extra shell).

Limiting Reactant & Percent Yield (71–73)

71. Grams of NaCl from 10.0 g Na + 10.0 g Cl₂?
2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl.
Na: 10.0 ÷ 22.99 = 0.435 mol (÷2 = 0.218)  |  Cl₂: 10.0 ÷ 70.90 = 0.141 mol (÷1 = 0.141) → Cl₂ limiting.
NaCl: 0.141 × 2 = 0.282 mol × 58.44 g/mol = 16.5 g NaCl
72. Mass of LiOH from 50.0 g Li + 45.0 g H₂O?
2Li + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂.
Li: 50.0 ÷ 6.94 = 7.20 mol  |  H₂O: 45.0 ÷ 18.02 = 2.50 mol → H₂O limiting (1:1 ratio).
LiOH: 2.50 mol × 23.95 g/mol = 59.8 g LiOH
73. % yield: 68.3 g H₂ + 85.4 g O₂ → 86.4 g H₂O collected?
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

Molarity (74–78)
